In [part 1](/blog/dissertation-revival-speed/) Claude got my 2018 code training 65× faster, so I wanted to see if today's methods could beat what I built as an undergrad.

My dissertation says my agent won 82.6% of Aliens games, but that was one run, measured while it was still training.[^2018] Retrained and tested properly, it won 73% of 500 games, scoring 47 points a game on average. The final agent won 99% of games, scoring 83 points on average.

## Step 1: which algorithm is best with more practice?

I tried three algorithms, each with 5 times the practice my agent got:

- **[DQN](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature14236):** DeepMind's 2015 Atari method, and my agent is this plus 2 tricks.
- **[Rainbow](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.02298):** DQN plus 6 tricks, including my 2.
- **[PPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347):** learns which move to make directly, instead of scoring each one.

### PPO actually wins more at 1M frames
With the same 1M frames as my agent, PPO wins 82% of the time, which is about what my dissertation said my agent won. However, it turns out it was just spamming the shoot button, which is why every chart has a dashed line at 82%: anything below it is worse than holding one button.

## Step 2: which reward works best?

So far it's just been the game's points, which can mislead, since some GVGAI games are [built so the score tricks agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.00048).[^clip] I tried:

- **Win only:** +1 for a win, −1 for a loss.
- **Win + points ÷ 10:** the same, plus a bit for scoring.
- **My 2018 reward:** points and win, plus −0.5 on every move of a lost game.

## Step 4: how often should it move?

In 2018 I wrote that holding each move for 4 frames meant my agent could never reach some states, but at 12 hours a run I couldn't afford to test it. A tree search called MCTS moves every frame and scores 78 with no training at all.[^mcts]

My 2018 agent won 73% of games. The final agent wins 99% and scores 83, even outscoring MCTS,[^mcts] which my 2018 agent never got close to.

So why not keep every frame, which won all 500 games? Because the test can't tell 99% from 100% apart from luck, and when it's a tie I keep the cheaper option.

Picking the winner after looking is how you fool yourself, which is why scientists [fix their rules up front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preregistration_%28science%29).

What I learned:

- Question the settings you copied: holding each move for 4 frames came from Atari and cost about 20 points here.
- Check the dumbest baseline first: holding one button needs no training, and I never tried it.
- Run everything more than once: my three 2018 runs ended up to 14 percentage points apart on win rate.

### Closing remarks

Take a look at the massive difference in speed. While reviving my dissertation I trained various agents, not just on Aliens but on other games in the GVGAI framework, and all those runs added up to about 4 days of training.

[^test]: [Welch's t-test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welch%27s_t-test) on the 5 runs: a gap counts as real when p < 0.05, so luck alone would give one that big less than 1 time in 20. The p-values are in [the results](https://github.com/ColinCee/GVGAI-Dissertation/blob/master/2026-revival/RESULTS.md).
[^mcts]: One run of 51 games, since it doesn't learn.
[^clip]: Each scoring move counts as 1, as in DeepMind's Atari setup.
[^2018]: Counted over training games 1,500 to 2,000, while it still made 1 in 10 moves at random.
